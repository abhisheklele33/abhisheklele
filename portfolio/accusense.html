Wearable Transcranial Magnetic Neuromodulation Cap for Motor Symptom Management in Parkinson's Disease

Overview

This project addresses the challenge of motor symptom management in Parkinson's disease (PD) — specifically tremor, rigidity, and bradykinesia — through a wearable transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) cap designed for home use. Conventional TMS therapy requires clinical visits to stationary equipment, severely limiting treatment accessibility and frequency for PD patients.

The proposed device integrates TMS coil elements into a comfortable, head-worn cap form factor, targeting specific cortical motor regions associated with PD motor dysfunction. The goal is to enable non-invasive, repeatable neuromodulation therapy that patients can self-administer, reducing reliance on dopaminergic pharmacotherapy and clinical appointment burden.

Parkinson's disease affects over 10 million people worldwide. Motor symptoms significantly impair quality of life, and current pharmacological treatments (levodopa) have dose-limiting side effects. Non-invasive neuromodulation offers a promising complementary approach.

Design Considerations
Stimulation Type
Repetitive TMS (rTMS)
Target Region
Primary motor cortex (M1)
Form Factor
Head-worn cap
Use Setting
Home / ambulatory
Safety
Non-invasive, closed-loop dosing
Symptoms Targeted
Tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia

Key design challenges include miniaturizing the TMS coil driver to wearable size while maintaining sufficient magnetic field strength, ensuring spatial targeting accuracy without neuronavigation infrastructure, and building safety interlocks to prevent overstimulation in an unsupervised home setting.

The cap uses a distributed coil array approach with modular positioning, allowing the stimulation focus to be adjusted based on the patient's anatomy. A closed-loop dosing protocol monitors session parameters and enforces safety limits automatically.

Clinical Context

TMS for Parkinson's disease has been studied extensively in clinical settings, with evidence supporting rTMS over M1 and supplementary motor area (SMA) for reducing motor symptom severity. The primary barrier to adoption is device size and treatment inconvenience. A wearable format could dramatically increase treatment frequency and thus cumulative therapeutic benefit.

This project focused on the device engineering and human factors design aspects, with reference to existing clinical TMS literature for parameter selection and the regulatory pathway considerations for eventual Class II or Class III device classification.
